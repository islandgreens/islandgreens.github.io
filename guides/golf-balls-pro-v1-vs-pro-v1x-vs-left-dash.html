Pro V1 vs. Pro V1x vs. Pro V1x Left Dash

The two balls everyone knows, and the one most golfers have never heard of, freshly rebuilt for 2026 after four years of development. The compression numbers alone tell a more interesting story than most golfers realize.

Pro V1 and Pro V1x are the two most played golf balls in the world, full stop. Left Dash is the quiet third option most golfers walk right past on the shelf, a Custom Performance Option that only became a mainstream retail ball in 2019 and just went through its first real update since. All three share the same urethane-covered, tour-grade DNA. Where they actually diverge is more specific, and less intuitive, than "firmer equals less spin."

Why the firmer ball spins more

This is the part of the Pro V1 family that trips people up, including plenty of golfers who've played these balls for years. The instinct is that firmer means less spin, since that pattern holds for most of the low-spin, high-swing-speed balls covered elsewhere on this site. Pro V1x breaks that pattern on purpose. Its extra mantle layer, the piece that makes it a four-piece dual-core ball instead of Pro V1's three-piece single core, is specifically engineered to add spin and workability into the green, not just to add speed. Independent testing backs this up directly: Pro V1x has produced 260 to 400 rpm more spin than Pro V1 on pitch shots, and roughly 10% more spin on full wedge shots, despite being the firmer, higher-compression ball of the two.

Off the tee, the two balls are close enough that most golfers won't notice a difference, testing consistently shows driver spin, launch, and carry within fractions of each other at normal swing speeds. The real gap shows up from about 150 yards in. A real-world example Titleist itself points to: Jake Knapp plays Pro V1 specifically because he already generates plenty of spin and wants the ball to calm it down, while Justin Thomas plays Pro V1x because he wants the extra spin and stopping power. Same swing speed range, opposite ball for opposite reasons.

The Left Dash refresh

Left Dash started life in 2018 as a tour-only Custom Performance Option, built for players who wanted Pro V1x-style height but with the spin pulled way down for a flatter, more penetrating flight. It reached retail in 2019 and then sat completely unchanged for eight years, an unusually long run for any Titleist product. The 2026 version is the first real update, and it took four years of development to get there.

The interesting part of that development story: Titleist's R&D team initially tested a softer, higher-spinning prototype, something closer to standard Pro V1x. Player feedback rejected it outright, spin rates on short irons were creeping too high, moving the ball away from the exact identity that made Left Dash players loyal to it in the first place. Titleist scrapped that direction and refocused entirely on what Left Dash was always supposed to be: speed, distance, and low spin, just more of each. The result touches every layer of the ball, a new higher-gradient dual core for more ball speed, a thicker high-flex casing layer to keep long-game spin down even as the core got faster, a new thinner urethane cover specifically to bring greenside spin and control closer in line with standard Pro V1 and Pro V1x, historically the weak point of the older Left Dash, and a new 348 tetrahedral dimple pattern for a flatter, more wind-stable flight.

Titleist is explicit that this isn't a ball for most golfers. It's the first-choice recommendation in roughly 5 to 8 percent of their fittings, aimed specifically at players who generate real speed and tend to launch the ball too high with too much spin. If that's not your miss, Left Dash won't outperform the other two. If it is, this is the one built around fixing exactly that. It's played across the PGA Tour, DP World Tour, Korn Ferry Tour, and several other global circuits, with past wins including the 2019 US Amateur and Bryson DeChambeau's 2024 US Open, though DeChambeau has since moved on to a different ball.

Island Greens Pick

Take Pro V1 if you already generate enough spin on your own and want the softest feel of the three. Take Pro V1x if you want more spin and stopping power on approach, particularly if your iron shots tend to release more than you'd like. Take Pro V1x Left Dash only if you genuinely launch the ball too high with too much long-game spin, Titleist's own numbers say that's a real fit for maybe one golfer in fifteen, not a ball to buy on curiosity alone.

From the bag

Pro V1 is always a staple for being a top ball in golf. It's hard to go wrong with that choice. It has that patented soft feel, but that's not something I personally love in my own game. I tried Pro V1x looking for that firmer ball, and I had real difficulty controlling it, I've since learned through research that the extra spin on that ball probably had something to do with that, which lines up exactly with what the numbers above show. I haven't been able to test Pro V1x Left Dash yet, so that one stays data-only for now.
